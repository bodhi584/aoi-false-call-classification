\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}
In electronics assembly, automated optical inspection (AOI) checks every solder joint after soldering and sends every flagged board to a manual inspection station. Most of these alarms are false calls: in public Siemens production data, about 99\,\% of the flagged solder joints are false calls. A second check with supervised machine learning after the AOI could release many false calls without manual inspection, and Siemens is working on this problem in its Erlangen electronics factory today.

This report reviews 18 sources, from the general to the specific. Earlier studies show that classifiers such as random forests can recognise false calls from AOI measurements. On the same Siemens data, however, models that meet the targets on a test set from the same period (at most 1\,\% missed defects, at least 40\,\% fewer manual checks) miss 5--15\,\% of the true defects on every later production period. The literature on dataset shift and concept drift, cases from manufacturing, and the authors of the data and of the study themselves point to drift, a change of the data over time, as the cause; an unstable method for setting the decision threshold adds to it. The standard remedy in the drift literature is to retrain the model regularly on recent data. In related applications, a binary fraud classifier with less than 1\,\% positives and a quality model on a semiconductor materials line, regular retraining reduced the loss in performance. For the second check of AOI results, this remedy has not been tested in the literature we reviewed.

Our project therefore reimplements the method of Pfab and Rothering with its own code, on the same data with the same models, and addresses the two causes they name: the threshold method and retraining. A design of experiments varies the threshold method (3 levels), the retraining schedule (6 levels), the retraining data (2 levels) and the model (2 levels). The deliverable is a simulation of the second check in production in which threshold and retraining are settings; its results are compared with the published result, and the targets of the original study (at most 1\,\% missed true defects, at least 40\,\% fewer manual checks) serve as reference lines.

\section{Introduction}
Zero-defect manufacturing relies on detecting defects before a product leaves the factory \cite{psarommatis2020}. In electronics assembly, components are soldered onto printed circuit boards by surface-mount technology (SMT), and AOI is placed after soldering \cite{ebayyeh2020}. AOI compares camera measurements of each solder joint with fixed limits; every flagged board goes to a manual inspection station, where an operator decides whether it is a true defect or a false call \cite{pfab2024}. Many flagged boards are false calls, which creates a large manual workload \cite{pfab2024,thielen2020}. A classifier after the AOI that releases false calls without missing true defects would reduce this workload. This review covers 18 sources from 2001 to 2026 on this second check; image-based defect detection is outside its scope, because the second check starts after the AOI has made its measurements. Figure~\ref{fig:map} shows the 18 sources from the general goal to our project; Section~2 follows it level by level.

\subsection{Motivation}
Every board that the AOI flags is checked by a person, and about 99\,\% of the flagged solder joints are false calls \cite{pfab2024,pfab2025}. This costs operator time on every line, and it is a current problem: Siemens is working on an AI second check in its Erlangen factory today, with the target of 40\,\% fewer false calls \cite{siemens2026}. Earlier studies show that a classifier on the AOI measurements can recognise many false calls \cite{thielen2020,albayari2026}. The only study that tests such a classifier on later production periods, however, finds that it fails there: it misses 5--15\,\% of the true defects instead of at most 1\,\% \cite{pfab2025}. A factory cannot use a second check that lets defects through. The authors name two possible fixes, a better method for setting the threshold and handling drift, but test neither. Figure~\ref{fig:benefit} shows the gap at a glance. The benefit is therefore large but not yet usable: on a test set from the same period the second check would save about 80--90\,\% of the manual checks within the 1\,\% limit, and even on later periods it still releases about 40--78\,\% of the false calls, but there it lets through 5--15\,\% of the true defects. If the two fixes bring the missed true defects back to 1\,\% while keeping at least 40\,\% of the false calls released, the manual inspection work on the line could fall by at least 40\,\%, the Siemens target. Our motivation is to find out whether these two fixes make the second check usable in real production. This review collects what the literature says about the problem, its causes and the possible fixes.

\subsubsection{Zero-defect manufacturing}
\textit{Level 1 of Figure~\ref{fig:map}, sources \cite{psarommatis2020}.}

The funnel starts with the most general goal, zero-defect manufacturing: defects should be found before a product leaves the factory. Psarommatis et al.\ reviewed 280 articles on this topic published between 1987 and 2018 and grouped the approaches into four strategies: detection, repair, prediction and prevention of defects \cite{psarommatis2020}. Inspection after a production step belongs to the detection strategy, and a second check after the inspection is a way to make detection cheaper without letting more defects pass. This leads to the next level: how defects are detected in electronics assembly.

\subsubsection{AOI in electronics assembly}
\textit{Level 2 of Figure~\ref{fig:map}, sources \cite{ebayyeh2020}.}

In electronics assembly, defects are detected after soldering by automated optical inspection (AOI). Abu Ebayyeh and Mousavi describe how such systems work: cameras and lighting record each board, and the software pre-processes the images, extracts features and classifies each solder joint against fixed limits \cite{ebayyeh2020}. AOI replaced human visual inspection, which is slow and suffers from fatigue. For our project, this means that the input of a second check is already given: the AOI has measured each joint, and the second check works on these measurements, not on the images. AOI, however, raises many alarms that turn out to be false; this is the next level.

\subsubsection{False calls of AOI}
\textit{Level 3 of Figure~\ref{fig:map}, sources \cite{siemens2026,pfab2024}.}

Most AOI alarms are false calls. That this is a current industrial problem is shown by a press release of Siemens from October 2026 \cite{siemens2026}. In its electronics factory in Erlangen, Siemens and NVIDIA prepare AOI programs on the computer before production, and when the AOI flags a possible defect, a second inspection step decides whether it is a real defect or a false call. The stated targets are up to 85\,\% less manual engineering effort, 40\,\% fewer false calls and more than 5\,\% higher first-pass yield. These are targets, not published results, but they show that the false-call problem is being worked on in industry today.

How large the problem is can be seen in data that Siemens published itself. Pfab et al.\ released 132 days of AOI data from one SMT line of Siemens AG in Germany \cite{pfab2024}. Every row is a solder joint that the AOI flagged, with its AOI measurements and the label from the manual inspection station: true defect or false call. The authors point out two limits of the data: the share of wrong labels is unknown, because people set them, and ``the data may contain distribution drifts of unknown type''. This is the data set of our project, and its authors already name drift as one of its properties. Our own first analysis confirms it (Figure~\ref{fig:drift}): the share of flagged boards with a true defect stays between 0.8 and 2.3\,\% for 15 weeks and rises to 3.2--6.3\,\% in the last four weeks.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{data_drift.png}
  \caption{Data of \cite{pfab2024}, our analysis: share of flagged boards with a true defect per production week (bars) and number of flagged boards (line).}
  \label{fig:drift}
\end{figure}

The obvious answer to so many false calls is a second check after the AOI, the next level.

\subsubsection{Second check with supervised machine learning}
\textit{Level 4 of Figure~\ref{fig:map}, sources \cite{thielen2020,albayari2026,pfab2025}.}

A second check with supervised machine learning decides for each flagged joint whether it is a true defect or a false call. Whether such a step can recognise false calls from the AOI measurements was first studied by Thielen et al.\ \cite{thielen2020}. They compared k-nearest neighbours, a neural network and random forests and varied the size of the data set and the decision threshold, with the aim of recognising as many false calls as possible without more missed defects. k-nearest neighbours worked best on small data sets but became too slow on large ones, where neural networks and random forests performed better. Their data are not public, and whether the test data come after the training data in time is not reported. Albayari et al.\ later used AOI data from Siemens AG and compared XGBoost, random forest, a deep neural network and k-nearest neighbours with several train--test splits \cite{albayari2026}. XGBoost gave the best balance of recall and precision, and the explanation methods LIME and SHAP showed which AOI measurements drive the predictions. Both studies therefore show that a second check can recognise false calls, but neither tests the classifier on production periods that come after the training data.

This is the gap that Pfab and Rothering close, and their study is the core paper of our project \cite{pfab2025}. On the data of \cite{pfab2024}, they split the rows in time order: the first half is used for modelling, with a stratified random 80/20 split into a tuning set and a test set, and the second half is cut into five later periods. Their goal is at most 1\,\% missed true defects (slip) and at least 40\,\% fewer manual checks (volume reduction). They tune k-nearest neighbours, random forests, balanced random forests, XGBoost and an AutoML model with Bayesian optimisation and 5-fold cross-validation; the decision threshold is the mean of the thresholds found in the five folds, and each model is trained once. Two models, a random forest and a balanced random forest, meet the goal on the test set, but on all five later periods they miss 5--15\,\% of the true defects (Figure~\ref{fig:pfab}). The authors conclude that the model ``would fail from the first moment in production''. A principal component analysis shows data clusters that ``did just appear in a certain period of time'', and the authors state that ``various distribution drifts'' occur in the data. As possible next steps they name ``improving the method for setting the threshold and handling the distribution drifts'', without testing either.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{pfab2025_slip.png}
  \caption{Missed true defects reported by Pfab and Rothering \cite{pfab2025} (Tables IV and V, mean $\pm$ SD of their runs): on target on the test set, far above it on all later periods.}
  \label{fig:pfab}
\end{figure}

Because the authors publish their code and run logs, their result can be checked directly. We reimplement their method with our own code as the baseline of our project and test the two next steps they name. Reading their code, we found that the stored threshold belongs to the last model tried during tuning, not to the best model that is finally used; in our reimplementation the threshold is taken from the best model. Two questions follow from this level: which classifiers to use, and how to evaluate them. They are the next two levels.

\subsubsection{Classifiers for tabular data}
\textit{Level 5 of Figure~\ref{fig:map}, sources \cite{grinsztajn2022,breiman2001,chen2004}.}

AOI measurements are tabular data: each joint is a row with numerical values and a few categories. Which classifier family works best on such data was studied by Grinsztajn et al.\ on 45 tabular data sets \cite{grinsztajn2022}. Tree-based models such as XGBoost and random forests remained state of the art for medium-sized data, even without counting their speed advantage. This supports the tree ensembles used in \cite{pfab2025} and means that deep tabular models are not needed to answer our question.

The two models of \cite{pfab2025} that we reimplement go back to two original works. Breiman introduced the random forest, a set of decision trees that are each trained on a random sample of the data with a random subset of features at each split and then vote \cite{breiman2001}; random forests reach an accuracy comparable to boosting, are robust to noise and give a measure of variable importance. Chen, Liaw and Breiman then proposed two variants for rare classes \cite{chen2004}: the weighted random forest, and the balanced random forest, which draws an equal number of cases from each class for every tree. The balanced variant fits our data, in which true defects are only about 1\,\% of the flagged joints. How these models should be evaluated when true defects are so rare is the next level.

\subsubsection{Evaluation with rare true defects}
\textit{Level 6 of Figure~\ref{fig:map}, sources \cite{he2009}.}

With so few true defects, the choice of measure matters. He and Garcia explain why accuracy is misleading when one class is rare: a model that calls every joint a false call would be right 99\,\% of the time and still miss every defect \cite{he2009}. They present class weighting and resampling as remedies and recommend class-wise measures such as recall and specificity. We therefore report, like \cite{pfab2025}, the missed true defects (1 $-$ recall) and the false calls released (specificity), and not accuracy. With the tools and the measures fixed, the remaining question is why the second check of \cite{pfab2025} fails on later periods.

\subsubsection{Why models fail over time: drift and threshold}
\textit{Level 7 of Figure~\ref{fig:map}, sources \cite{gama2014,bauer2025,tong2018}.}

Pfab and Rothering name two causes for the failure on later periods: drift and the method for setting the threshold \cite{pfab2025}. Drift is covered by the literature on concept drift. Gama et al.\ define concept drift as a change of the joint distribution $p(X, y)$ over time and group the ways of adapting to it into memory (which data to keep or forget), change detection and learning \cite{gama2014}. One learning mode is ``retraining that discards the current model and builds a new model from scratch'', and a fixed sliding window that periodically retrains on the latest examples is ``often used as a baseline''. Periodic retraining on recent data is therefore the standard remedy against drift, and it is the remedy that is missing in \cite{pfab2025}.

That drift alone lowers the performance of a quality classifier in manufacturing was shown in a controlled experiment by Bauer et al.\ \cite{bauer2025}. For the optical quality monitoring of friction stir welds (good or not good), they tested the same models on normal data and on data with controlled drift: other tool geometries, alloys and sheet thicknesses, more brightness and blur. All metrics dropped on the drifted data. Together with the warning in \cite{pfab2024} and the data clusters in \cite{pfab2025}, this makes drift the most likely main cause of the failure over time.

The second cause is the threshold. Tong, Feng and Li show why the usual way of setting it is not enough \cite{tong2018}. When one type of error must stay below a bound $\alpha$, the common practice of limiting the \textit{empirical} error to $\alpha$ ``does not satisfy the [\ldots] error control objective'', because the resulting classifiers ``are still likely to have [\ldots] errors much larger than $\alpha$''. A threshold set once on old data therefore cannot be trusted to keep the limit of at most 1\,\% missed true defects. Both causes have known remedies, which form the last level.

\subsubsection{Methods: threshold and retraining}
\textit{Level 8 of Figure~\ref{fig:map}, sources \cite{tong2018,saerens2002,gama2004,dalpozzolo2018,gao2026}.}

\textbf{Threshold.} For the first next step named in \cite{pfab2025}, ``improving the method for setting the threshold'', two methods exist. The first comes from the same work of Tong et al.\ \cite{tong2018}. Their umbrella algorithm works with any scoring classifier, including random forests: it takes the threshold as an order statistic of the scores of a left-out sample and so guarantees that the error stays below $\alpha$ with probability at least $1-\delta$. This needs a minimum size of the left-out sample, $n \geq \log\delta / \log(1-\alpha)$. For our bound of at most 1\,\% missed true defects, with $\alpha = 0.01$ and $\delta = 0.05$, the left-out data must contain at least 299 true defects; with fewer, no threshold can guarantee the bound (Figure~\ref{fig:np}). In the data of \cite{pfab2024}, true defects are about 1\,\% of the flagged joints, so 299 true defects need roughly 30,000 flagged joints. The cross-validation folds of \cite{pfab2025} are large enough for this, but a short recent period may not be. This explains why a threshold for 1\,\% slip is unstable, and it gives a method, a threshold with a safety margin, to set it with a stated confidence.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{np_sample_size.png}
  \caption{Minimum number of true defects in the left-out data so that the threshold keeps the share of missed true defects below $\alpha$ with the stated confidence, $n \geq \log\delta/\log(1-\alpha)$ \cite{tong2018}; computed by us.}
  \label{fig:np}
\end{figure}

The second method is needed when the share of true defects changes, as it does in the last weeks of the Siemens data (Figure~\ref{fig:drift}). Saerens, Latinne and Decaestecker show that when the share of a class in use differs from its share in training, the outputs of a classifier no longer fit \cite{saerens2002}. They re-estimate the new class shares from new, unlabelled data and adjust the outputs. For our project this means that a threshold fixed once cannot follow a changing share of true defects; it has to be set again for each period.

\textbf{Retraining.} For the second next step, ``handling the distribution drifts within the dataset'' \cite{pfab2025}, retraining is the basic remedy \cite{gama2014}; the open questions are when to retrain and whether it helps in practice. Gama et al.\ proposed the drift detection method (DDM), which retrains only when needed \cite{gama2004}. It tracks the online error rate of the model; a warning level is reached when the error plus its standard deviation exceeds the lowest value seen by two standard deviations, and a drift level at three. At a drift, a new model is learned from the examples since the warning. This is the alternative to retraining on a fixed schedule.

Evidence that regular retraining helps comes from two fields close to ours. In credit card fraud detection, Dal Pozzolo et al.\ studied a binary task with fewer than 1\,\% positives on a stream of more than 75 million transactions over three years \cite{dalpozzolo2018}. The classifier is updated every day. A classifier trained on the most recent days ranks frauds better than one trained on data a week older, which the authors take as evidence that the stream is non-stationary and that recent data are more informative; their strategy that uses the newest labelled transactions performs best throughout. This is the closest classification analogue to our task: binary, rare positives, a long daily stream, and regular retraining that helps.

In manufacturing, Gao et al.\ studied five years of data from a semiconductor materials line, where a random forest that predicts product quality degrades after a supplier change and with equipment wear \cite{gao2026}. Retraining every 100 batches gave ``only marginal improvements'', while retraining every 5 batches gave about 80\,\% fewer out-of-range predictions. The retraining frequency mattered more than the choice between a fixed and a growing training window, and new hyper-parameter tuning at each retraining was not worth its cost. The authors prefer a fixed schedule to drift-triggered retraining, which ``will only be triggered after poor predictions have been observed''. Their task is regression, not classification, and the study is a preprint, but it shows that in a real production setting the frequency of retraining is the setting that matters most.

\subsubsection{Bottom line: our project}
\textit{Bottom line of Figure~\ref{fig:map}.}

Read from top to bottom, the funnel narrows the general goal of zero-defect manufacturing down to one open question. AOI detects defects but raises many false calls (levels 1--3). A second check with machine learning removes many of them on a test set, but fails on later production days (level 4). Tree models and class-wise measures are the right tools (levels 5 and 6). Drift and a threshold set once explain the failure (level 7), and methods for both exist and have worked in other fields (level 8). For the second check of AOI results, no study has tested them yet. Our project therefore uses the threshold method and retraining as the variables of a simulation and compares every setting with \cite{pfab2025}.

% ---------------- Discussion ----------------
\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the literature shows}
Three points are well supported. First, false calls are a large and current cost in electronics assembly: about 99\,\% of the flagged joints in the Siemens data are false calls \cite{pfab2024,pfab2025}, and Siemens is working on a second check in production today \cite{siemens2026}. Second, a classifier on the AOI measurements can recognise many false calls: three independent studies show this with tree ensembles, k-nearest neighbours and neural networks \cite{thielen2020,albayari2026,pfab2025}, which agrees with the general finding that tree ensembles are strong on tabular data \cite{grinsztajn2022}. Third, a good result on a test set from the same period does not carry over to later production: the only study that tests later periods finds 5--15\,\% missed true defects where the target is 1\,\% \cite{pfab2025}.

\subsection{Why the earlier results look better than they are}
Most studies evaluate on a random or stratified split of data from the same period \cite{thielen2020,albayari2026}. Under drift, such a split mixes past and future and hides the loss in performance \cite{gama2014}. The evaluation in time order of \cite{pfab2025} is therefore the one that matches the use in a factory, and it is the reason why we build on this study. The measure matters as well: with about 1\,\% true defects, accuracy says little, and recall and specificity must be reported for the class that matters \cite{he2009}.

\subsection{What the literature leaves open}
For the second check of AOI results, no reviewed study tests a remedy against the failure over time. The two causes are named \cite{pfab2025}, and general methods exist for both: a threshold with a stated confidence \cite{tong2018}, a threshold that follows a changed class share \cite{saerens2002}, and retraining on a schedule or on detected drift \cite{gama2004,gama2014}. Evidence that retraining helps comes from other fields: fraud detection, a binary task with rare positives \cite{dalpozzolo2018}, and a semiconductor materials line, a regression task \cite{gao2026}. Whether the same holds for AOI data, with about 1\,\% true defects and a target of at most 1\,\% missed defects, is open. This gap is the research question of our project.

\subsection{Limitations of the reviewed sources}
Two of the central sources are preprints \cite{pfab2025,gao2026}; for \cite{pfab2025}, the published code and run logs allow us to check the results ourselves. The industrial targets of Siemens \cite{siemens2026} are targets, not measured results. The manufacturing evidence for retraining \cite{gao2026} concerns regression, not classification, and the drift experiment of \cite{bauer2025} works on images. The labels of the Siemens data come from operators and may contain errors \cite{pfab2024}. Finally, all evidence for the AOI case comes from one production line; whether the findings transfer to other lines is not known.

% ---------------- Conclusion ----------------
\section{Conclusion}
The literature leads from a general goal to one specific question. Zero-defect manufacturing needs reliable defect detection \cite{psarommatis2020}; in electronics assembly this is done by AOI \cite{ebayyeh2020}, which raises many false calls that operators check by hand \cite{pfab2024}, a problem that industry is still working on \cite{siemens2026}. Supervised classifiers after the AOI can recognise false calls \cite{thielen2020,albayari2026}, and on tabular data like the AOI measurements tree ensembles are the strongest family \cite{grinsztajn2022}.

On the same public data that our project uses, however, Pfab and Rothering \cite{pfab2025} show that models which meet the targets on a test set from the same period miss 5--15\,\% of the true defects on every later period. The evidence points to drift as the main cause: by definition, a model fails when the data it meets differ from the data it learned from \cite{gama2014}; a controlled manufacturing experiment shows this effect \cite{bauer2025}; and for the Siemens data the authors themselves warn of drift and show data clusters that appear only in certain periods \cite{pfab2024,pfab2025}. The method for setting the threshold, named by \cite{pfab2025} as a second problem, makes it worse: a threshold set on the empirical error does not keep a 1\,\% bound unless enough true defects are left out for it \cite{tong2018}, and a threshold fixed once does not follow a changing class share \cite{saerens2002}.

The standard remedy for drift is to retrain the model regularly on recent data \cite{gama2014}. It has helped in a binary classification task with rare positives \cite{dalpozzolo2018} and on a semiconductor materials line, where the retraining frequency mattered most \cite{gao2026}. For the second check of AOI results, the reviewed literature does not report whether it works.

\textbf{Position of our project.} In their discussion, Pfab and Rothering name as ``potential next steps to stabilize the modeling'' the ``improving [of] the method for setting the threshold and handling the distribution drifts within the dataset'' \cite{pfab2025}, without testing either. Our project focuses on exactly these two steps. We do not propose a new method; we test whether the standard remedies work for this real problem. First, we reimplemented the method of \cite{pfab2025} with our own code, on the same data, with the same split, models, hyper-parameters and threshold method; while doing so we corrected one point: the threshold is taken from the best model of the tuning, not from the last model tried. This reimplementation is our baseline. Second, a design of experiments varies the threshold method (as in \cite{pfab2025} corrected, a safety margin \cite{tong2018}, re-set per period \cite{saerens2002}), the retraining schedule (never, per period, weekly, every 3 days, daily, on drift \cite{gama2004}), the retraining data (all data so far or a recent window \cite{gama2014}) and the model (random forest or balanced random forest), each setting with the ten seeds of \cite{pfab2025}. The question is: how do missed true defects and released false calls on later production periods change when threshold and retraining change, compared with training once? Every result is shown next to the published result of \cite{pfab2025}; its targets (at most 1\,\% missed true defects, at least 40\,\% fewer manual checks) serve as reference lines. In the end, the simulation becomes a small software tool in which threshold and retraining are settings. Whatever the result, it answers a question that the original study left open.
